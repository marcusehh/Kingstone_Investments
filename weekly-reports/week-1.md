**Healthcare**  
The healthcare sector continues to assert itself as the portfolio's defensive play amidst unprecedented market volatility, benefitting from capital rotation out of concentrated areas within the technology sector, notably AI. The sector is broadly characterised by an ageing global population, rising chronic disease prevalence, the recurrence of medical needs and continual technological innovation. Its reputation as a defensive haven is reflected in the S\&P 500 Healthcare Index's modest rise over the past 5 days by 0.89%, and more significantly over the last month, by 5.63%, outpacing the S\&P over both of these time frames (0.44% and 2.42% respectively) and reinforcing the significance of this sector as a strong investment option.  
Regulatory momentum has been accelerating significantly in the healthcare industry, most recently evidenced by the Medicine and Healthcare Regulatory Agency (MHRA), the UK's national regulatory body for all healthcare-related products, committing to reducing commercial drug trial approval times to enhance patient access to novel medicines. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved around 30 novel drugs and biological therapeutics year-to-date, exceeding 2024 and 2025's pace, including several blockbuster treatments such as Takeda's Orzeyful, a first-in-class oral treatment for narcolepsy type 1, and Moderna's mRNA-based flu vaccine. Speculation surrounding consolidation of the healthcare industry serves to reiterate just how big a player it truly is. Whilst the approximately $400 billion prospective mega deal between AstraZeneca and Bristol Myers Squibb crumbled at the last hour, it points to an emerging theme: an increasing willingness by mid-to-large cap pharmaceutical companies to pool resources to scale and leverage combined AI-enabled R\&D capabilities.

**Industrials**  
Industrials ended the week with a very slight rise, broadly tracking the wider market as the S\&P 500 climbed 0.4% over the week, with the Dow down 0.6% and the Nasdaq up 0.1%, whilst the small-cap Russell 2000 closed at a new all-time high on reduced rate-hike concerns which is a supportive backdrop for the US cyclicals. The sector benchmark XLI closed Friday at $186.49, within roughly 1% of its 52-week high of $188.19, so industrials have returned more than 16% year-to-date, ranking among the top-performing sector funds, driven by massive AI infrastructure spending, defence demand, reshoring and improving manufacturing activity. Earnings momentum remains a sector-wide tailwind, with 87% of the roughly 455 S\&P 500 companies reporting so far having beaten EPS estimates, and industrials capturing early AI-cycle capital expenditure through data-centre construction. A re-escalation of hike expectations into Jackson Hole would be the main downside risk to the sector's rate-sensitive momentum.

**Energy & Utilities**  
Global grids are straining under the same forces everywhere. Middle East tensions are keeping Strait of Hormuz transits severely constrained, with the EIA expecting disruptions of roughly 0.6 million barrels a day to persist through 2027\. Meanwhile the grid-vs-demand squeeze that hit Texas is playing out worldwide. ERCOT set a new peak load record of 91.1 GW in July, 6% above its prior high, met mostly by gas and solar. Renewables are covering more load in markets like India while data centres force regulators everywhere to separate real demand from speculative demand. Europe is adding capacity fast too, with the EU approving a €63bn French scheme for offshore wind and Poland's Baltic Power delivering first grid power at 1.2 GW.  
For investors, it's a global re-rating, not just a US story. Global utilities are up broadly this year: European utilities \+10.5% YTD, Asia \+9.4%, US \+7.5%, continuing a run that saw a 20% gain in 2025\. Generalist European investors who ignored the sector last year are now piling in as a hedge against AI disruption elsewhere, pushing European utilities to 9.0x forward EV/EBITDA, above their historical range. The easy money has been made. Going forward, PwC frames the dealmaking thesis as energy security, affordability, and efficiency, with buyers chasing power, grid, and gas assets that can deliver capacity faster than new-build projects amid geopolitical instability.

**Real Estate**  
Global institutional appetite continued to broaden, with global living sector investment in H1 2026 up roughly 9% year on year across the Americas, EMEA and Asia Pacific, while office leasing rose to a new post-pandemic high led by the US, and industrial take-up increased across most major markets. Logistics and data centre infrastructure remain the standout theme: data centre construction capacity is forecast to grow 19% in 2026 as hyperscalers commit record capital, even as multi-housing development in the US sits more than three quarters below its recent peak. In the US, Freddie Mac's 30-year fixed averaged 6.67% for the week to 13 August, down from 6.69% the prior week, and total mortgage application volume rose 3.6% week on week as rates eased slightly after five consecutive weeks of increases. Fannie Mae and the MBA have since walked back earlier forecasts of rates nearing 6% by year end, now guiding to 6.3% to 6.5% given sticky inflation and elevated Treasury yields.

Europe's largest listed real estate event of the year crystallised this week with Prologis and SEGRO's £14.3bn merger finalising terms, a deal emblematic of the wider European tone: 2026 sentiment has shifted from cautious optimism to something more pragmatic, with investors becoming increasingly selective toward cities and sectors with strong fundamentals amid ongoing geopolitical uncertainty. Structural obsolescence risk remains a live theme globally, with the ten largest office markets for repositioning collectively having over 130 million square metres of space at risk of stranding, and Paris, London, New York, Boston and Chicago offering some of the most compelling retrofit opportunities. In the US, housing investors are describing this as their worst market in at least three years even as branded residential remains a bright spot, with luxury branded residences now representing a $67 billion global boom. Capital is bifurcating hard by conviction. Logistics, data centres and repositioning plays are attracting record commitment, while core residential and generalist office exposure stay under pressure from rates and thin transaction volumes.

**FX**  
The US dollar weakened against most G10 currencies in the week to 14 August as inflation data reduced pressure for an immediate Federal Reserve rate increase. July headline CPI rose 0.1% month-on-month and 3.4% year-on-year, while producer prices were unchanged and the measure excluding food and energy increased 0.2%, both slightly below expectations. The figures supported the view that the Fed can remain patient, although elevated energy costs mean that inflation risks have not disappeared. Sterling also benefited from resilient UK activity: GDP expanded 0.4% in the second quarter and 0.3% in June.

Elsewhere, the Reserve Bank of Australia unanimously held its cash rate at 4.35% but signalled it could still raise rates if needed, helping the Australian dollar join the broader advance against the US dollar. The yen remained volatile and did not build on its intervention-led gains, suggesting that interest-rate gaps and energy-import costs continue to limit appreciation. The Canadian dollar also benefited from general dollar weakness, but downside risk remains significant because additional targeted US duties on selected Canadian products are still scheduled to take effect on 19 August. Overall, the week modestly weakens the near-term dollar outlook, while supporting continued caution on CAD and treating any sustained JPY recovery as dependent on narrower rate differentials.

**Macro**  
This week was about inflation expectations getting re-priced after last week's payrolls miss (-23k). CPI Wednesday and PPI Thursday were the test of whether the market's shift toward a September pause was justified. Both came in soft, so 2yr yields fell as hike probability was pulled out of the front end. Equities followed: lower expected short rates mean a lower discount rate on forward earnings, multiples expand, and the S\&P broke 7,800 for the first time.

Friday's the one worth sitting with. Retail sales missed hard (-0.6% vs \+0.1% exp), which should have pulled yields lower across the curve on a growth-slowdown read. Instead 10s and 30s backed up. The driver was oil: Hormuz tensions kept crude bid, which fed straight into breakeven inflation and reasserted a term premium at the long end that the front end wasn't pricing at all. That's a curve decoupling: 2s pricing "Fed's done hiking," 10s/30s pricing "inflation risk isn't fully retired." It's also why equities faded into the close despite the dovish data, higher real/nominal long yields hit the discount rate on long-duration growth names even as short-end pricing stayed supportive. The 2s10s (or even 2s30s) relationship is doing the actual work of telling you whether the market believes the disinflation trade or is just hedging against it.
