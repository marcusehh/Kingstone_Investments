Real estate is having its best year in nearly a decade. The Morningstar US Real Estate Index has climbed 16.4% year-to-date through the end of July: on track to be the first year the sector has outperformed the broader market in over ten years. In the first quarter alone, when the S\&P 500 fell 4.3%, real estate returned 3.8%. The gap between the two was one of the widest in the post-2008 era.

For an industry that has spent three years in the shadow of rising interest rates, this feels like vindication. And in many ways, it is. But the headline numbers are telling only half the story.

**Where the money went**  
The winners of 2026 are easy to identify, and they share a common thread. Healthpeak Properties, a life sciences and healthcare REIT, returned 47.4% year-to-date. Hotel and resort REITs returned an average of 42.8% for the first half, powered in part by FIFA World Cup demand. Welltower, the world's largest healthcare REIT, gained 32.1% and was the single biggest contributor to the sector's index performance. Data centre REITs \- Equinix, Digital Realty, and Iron Mountain \- collectively accounted for approximately 27.8% of the entire sector's total returns.

Martin Szumski, a Morningstar equity analyst, put it plainly: AI is "the biggest thing that has happened to the real estate industry since the internet." The six largest US hyperscalers are projected to spend approximately $700 billion in capital expenditure in 2026, nearly six times their 2022 levels, and data centre construction has become the single largest segment of US commercial building, surpassing traditional office for the first time.

The macro picture helped too. Investors rotating out of a volatile technology sector found real estate's predictable, contractual cash flows increasingly attractive. A wave of mergers and acquisitions, with H1 2026 REIT deal volume reaching approximately $68.8 billion, including the largest REIT merger in the modern era between Equity Residential and AvalonBay, sent a clear signal: private capital believes public market valuations are under-pricing these assets by as much as 19%.

**The sector the rally forgot**  
Against all of this, one sub-sector fell sharply in Q1 while everything around it rose. Office REITs declined 12.8% in the first quarter alone. This is not a lag or a temporary dislocation. It reflects a structural reality that improving leasing activity in premium buildings cannot paper over.

National office vacancy in the United States sits at 19.4%. The Trepp CMBS office delinquency rate reached 11.76% in April 2026, an all-time record, above the peak reached during the 2008 financial crisis. Of the $21.3 billion in office CMBS loans that matured before the end of 2026, 83.7% were already showing delinquencies at the time of maturity.

The structural problem is a debt problem, not an occupancy problem. Between 2020 and 2022, near-zero interest rates fuelled an extraordinary borrowing cycle across commercial real estate. Loans were originated at 3-4%. Those same loans are now maturing into a refinancing market where the equivalent rate is 5.5-6.5%. According to S\&P Global Market Intelligence, $1.26 trillion in CRE loans will mature in 2027 alone, more than three times the 20-year average. Annual maturities will remain above $1 trillion every year through 2030\.

**The safety valve is failing**  
The standard reassurance about this maturity wall has always been that private credit, the $3 trillion shadow lending sector that expanded to fill the gap left by banks retreating under regulatory pressure after 2008, would absorb the refinancing failures that traditional lenders could not handle. In 2026, that assumption is being tested publicly for the first time.

In February, Blue Owl Capital gated withdrawals from a retail credit vehicle. An Apollo-managed business development company cut its dividend and marked down assets. Blackstone's private credit fund raised its repurchase cap to meet nearly $2 billion in simultaneous redemptions. Shares of Blue Owl fell approximately 40% year-to-date; KKR, Apollo, and Blackstone each fell more than 20%. The Federal Reserve began formally requesting data from major banks on their private credit exposure. The US Treasury convened one-on-one meetings with industry leaders.

Approximately $100 billion in opportunistic distressed capital has been raised specifically to step into CRE stress situations. Against a $1.26 trillion maturity wall, that represents roughly 8% coverage. It is capital rationing. It is not a solution.  
   
**What comes next**   
The second half of 2026 carries a dense catalyst calendar. Q2 REIT earnings from mid-July through to mid-August will be the first reporting period where the 2026 maturity acceleration is visible in the numbers. Regional bank earnings will surface the extent to which loan loss provisions are rising beyond consensus estimates. Monthly CMBS delinquency data from Trepp will track whether office stress is accelerating beyond the current 11.76% record.

The broader sector recovery is real and deserves recognition. Demographics, AI infrastructure, and supply constraints in living and healthcare sectors are structural tailwinds that will not reverse. But the recovery is not uniform, and the distinction between the winners and the losers has rarely been more consequential.

In commercial real estate in 2026, the question is not whether you own the sector, it is which part of the sector you own and whether the debt those assets are carrying can survive the next eighteen months of refinancing reality.
